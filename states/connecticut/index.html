Recently enacted

Convertible pistols ban (June 2026)

In June 2026, Governor Lamont signed a new law (originating as HB 5043, "An Act Concerning Convertible Pistols") banning the future sale and importation of semiautomatic pistols with a cruciform trigger bar that can be readily converted into a machine gun using a common household tool and an attached "switch." Violating the law is a class D felony.

Source: Governor's press release, June 2026

Permits and purchasing

  • A pistol permit is required to purchase, possess, or carry a pistol or revolver in Connecticut. The permit costs $70 and is valid for five years. Applicants must complete a handgun safety course that includes live fire — the NRA's "Basic Pistol Course" is the minimum standard; dry-fire-only or simulator courses don't qualify.

    Source: CT DESPP — State Pistol Permit

  • A Long Gun Eligibility Certificate is required to purchase a rifle or shotgun, and a separate Handgun Eligibility Certificate exists for those who want to purchase a pistol without going through the full carry-permit process. Both simply authorize purchase and possession, not carrying.

    Source: CT DESPP — Pistol Permit FAQ

  • Connecticut uses a two-step process for a first-time pistol permit: a 60-day temporary permit issued by your local police chief or first selectman, followed by the 5-year state permit issued by DESPP's Special Licensing and Firearms Unit. Renewals go directly to DESPP.

    Source: CT DESPP — Firearms and Permit Forms

  • Permit holders must notify DESPP of an address change within two business days.

    Source: CT DESPP — Pistol Permit FAQ

Where you can carry

  • With a valid Connecticut State Pistol Permit, you may carry pistols and revolvers statewide. The permit allows an unlimited number of handguns and is valid for five years. You must carry the permit on your person whenever you are carrying a handgun — failure to do so is an infraction ($35 fine).

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-33b, § 29-35, § 29-37(c) — summarized in CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0001

  • Connecticut does not require concealed carry specifically, but open carry in public is restricted. Public Act 23-53 (effective October 1, 2023) generally prohibits knowingly carrying a firearm with the intent to display it in public. Exceptions include your dwelling, premises you own or lease, and a business you own.

    Source: CGA OLR — Major Public Acts of 2023 (PA 23-53), Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-35

  • A pistol permit is not a blanket authorization to carry everywhere. State law provides that issuance of a permit does not authorize carrying on any premises where possession is prohibited by law or by the person who owns or controls the premises.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-28(e)

  • No permit is required to possess lawfully acquired handguns in your home or at your place of business. Eligibility certificates authorize purchase and possession but do not authorize carrying in public — a full pistol permit is required for that.

    Source: CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0001, DESPP — Pistol Permit FAQ

Where you cannot carry

  • Schools and school activities. It is generally illegal to possess a firearm on the grounds of a public or private elementary or secondary school, or at a school-sponsored activity (on or off school property), unless you are licensed or privileged to do so. Violations are a class D felony.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 53a-217b — see also CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0354

  • State Capitol and legislative buildings. Firearms are prohibited in any building where either chamber of the General Assembly is located, where a legislator or legislative employee has an official office, or where a legislative committee is holding a public hearing. Violations are a class D felony (police, military on duty, and honor-guard veterans are exempt).

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 2-1e(c)

  • State parks and forests. DEEP regulations prohibit hunting or carrying firearms, archery equipment, or other weapons in any state park or forest except as authorized by the department. All weapon use must comply with applicable Connecticut statutes.

    Source: Conn. Agencies Regs. § 23-4-1(c) — DEEP State Park Rules

  • Private property and other premises. You may not carry a handgun on any premises where the owner or person in control has prohibited firearms, or where another statute prohibits them. Violating permit conditions in a prohibited location can result in fines, imprisonment up to three years, and forfeiture of the handgun.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-28(e), § 29-37

  • Federal locations. Federal law independently restricts firearms in places such as federal courthouses, post offices, military installations, and airport secure areas — regardless of a Connecticut permit. Always check facility-specific rules before entering.

    Source: CT Judicial Branch Law Library — Firearms (statute index)

  • Loaded handguns in vehicles (without a permit). If you do not have a valid pistol permit, a handgun being transported in a motor vehicle must be unloaded and kept where it is not readily or directly accessible from the passenger compartment, or in a locked container other than the glove compartment or console.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-35 — summarized in CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0001

Private property & signage

  • Connecticut is not a "posted sign = automatic crime" state in the way some other states operate. There is no Connecticut statute that makes a specific "no firearms" sign by itself a standalone criminal offense for a permit holder. Instead, the key statute is § 29-28(e): your pistol permit does not authorize you to carry on premises where the owner or person in control prohibits firearms.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-28(e), CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0001

  • In practice, private businesses, landlords, and property owners may prohibit firearms as a condition of entry. A posted policy or verbal notice that firearms are not allowed means carrying there violates your permit's scope — even if no special sign format is required by statute.

    Source: CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0001 — "Carrying Handguns where Prohibited"

  • If you remain on private property after the owner or an authorized person personally orders you to leave, you may face criminal trespass charges — first degree if you know you are not privileged to be there (class A misdemeanor), or second degree for remaining without privilege (class B misdemeanor).

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. §§ 53a-107, 53a-108 — see CGA OLR Report 2002-R-0365

  • Municipalities may also regulate firearms on town-owned property under their general police powers, though authority varies by situation. When in doubt, contact the property owner or municipality before carrying.

    Source: CGA OLR Report 98-R-0785

Reciprocity

  • Connecticut does not recognize other states' carry permits. There are no reciprocity agreements allowing out-of-state permit holders to carry in Connecticut without a Connecticut permit. Anyone carrying a handgun in Connecticut must hold a valid Connecticut pistol permit, with limited statutory exceptions (home, business, transport, etc.).

    Source: CGA OLR Report 2007-R-0116 — "Reciprocity Agreements for Gun Permits"

  • Out-of-state residents can apply for a Connecticut permit. Non-residents apply directly to DESPP's Special Licensing and Firearms Unit. A prerequisite is holding a valid permit or license to carry from another U.S. jurisdiction. Request an application by emailing SLFU.OOS@ct.gov with your full name and mailing address.

    Source: DESPP — Pistol Permit FAQ, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-28(f)

  • Connecticut permit holders traveling to other states must check each destination state's laws. Connecticut has no outbound reciprocity agreements — whether another state honors a Connecticut permit is entirely up to that state and changes over time. DESPP does not publish a reciprocity map.

    Source: CGA OLR Report 2007-R-0116

  • Passing through Connecticut without a CT permit. You may transport unloaded handguns through Connecticut for lawful purposes if you are traveling between states where you may legally possess and carry, you are not otherwise prohibited, and the firearm is kept where it is not readily accessible or in a locked container (not the glove compartment or console). See the Transport section below for details.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-38d

Transport in vehicles

  • Permit holders. If you have a valid Connecticut pistol permit, you may carry a loaded handgun on your person in a vehicle. You must have your permit with you. This is standard permit-based carry, not a separate "vehicle carry" rule.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-35, DESPP — Pistol Permit FAQ

  • Handguns without a permit (or being transported, not carried). Handguns in a motor vehicle must be unloaded and kept in a location not readily or directly accessible from the passenger compartment. If the vehicle has no separate compartment, the handgun must be in a locked container other than the glove compartment or console. Violations carry serious penalties including a mandatory minimum one-year sentence in some circumstances.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-35, § 29-37 — summarized in CGA OLR Report 2013-R-0001

  • Rifles and shotguns. Long guns transported in a vehicle or snowmobile must be unloaded. Certain exemptions apply for military, law enforcement, and security personnel in the performance of their duties.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 53-205

  • Non-residents passing through Connecticut. Under § 29-38d, you may transport unloaded handguns through Connecticut without a Connecticut permit if you are traveling between states where you may legally possess them, you are not otherwise prohibited, and the firearm is stored as required (not readily accessible, or locked away from the passenger area). You may not use, carry, sell, or transfer the firearm while in the state.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-38d, CGA OLR Report 2007-R-0116

  • Non-residents for specific purposes. Bona fide non-residents who hold a carry permit in their home state may transport handguns in Connecticut without a Connecticut permit for limited purposes: competitions, formal training at an approved range, repair, or organized gun-collector exhibitions — subject to the statutory conditions in § 29-30.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-30

Safe storage law

Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-37i

  • State law requires anyone who stores or keeps a firearm to either keep it in a securely locked container/location, or keep it on their person (or close enough to immediately retrieve and use), if they know or reasonably should know that: a minor could access it without a parent/guardian's permission, a resident of the home is legally ineligible to possess a firearm, or a resident poses a risk of imminent injury to themselves or others.

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-37i, current text — statute index also listed at CT Judicial Branch Law Library

  • "Minor" is currently defined as anyone under 18 (this was raised from under-16 by a 2019 amendment).

    Source: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 29-37i, current text

Magazine capacity

  • A "large capacity magazine" is defined as any magazine, belt, drum, feed strip, or similar device that holds — or can be readily restored/converted to hold — more than 10 rounds.

    Source: CT General Assembly, Office of Legislative Research — 2024-R-0163

  • The sale and purchase ban took effect April 5, 2013. Magazines lawfully owned before that date had to be declared to DESPP by January 1, 2014 to remain legally possessed.

    Source: CT General Assembly, Office of Legislative Research — 2024-R-0163

Assault weapons law

Conn. Gen. Stat. § 53-202a et seq.

  • State law generally prohibits possessing, selling, or transferring an "assault weapon" as defined by statute, with an exception for owners who lawfully possessed the firearm before the April 4, 2013 ban and obtained a certificate of possession from DESPP.

    Source: CT General Assembly, Office of Legislative Research — 2013-R-0241, corroborated by DESPP's FAQ on P.A. 13-3

  • The statutory definition covers: any selective-fire firearm capable of automatic, semi-automatic, or burst fire; more than 150 specifically named semiautomatic rifles and pistols; and semiautomatic firearms meeting certain feature-based tests (e.g., a detachable magazine that attaches outside the pistol grip, combined with other listed features).

    Source: CT General Assembly, Office of Legislative Research — 2013-R-0241
